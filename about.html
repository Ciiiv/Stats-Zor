How it works

Every number on Stats-Zor is computed from public data by code you can read in the GitHub repo. Nothing is hand-entered except the cap figures listed under Value and worth. This page explains, section by section, what each part shows, how it's calculated, why it's built that way, and what it can't see.

Glossary

5v5
Five skaters a side, no power play. Most even-strength stats here are 5v5 only, because special teams change everything about shot rates.
xG (expected goals)
MoneyPuck's estimate of how likely each shot is to score, from its location, type, angle, rebound and rush status. Adding them up measures chance quality better than goals, which swing on luck and goaltending.
xGF / xGA
Expected goals for and against while the player is on the ice.
xGF%
xGF ÷ (xGF + xGA). 50% is break-even; above 50% means his team controls the chances while he's out there.
Per 60
A rate per 60 minutes of ice time, so players with different minutes can be compared fairly.
Relative (rel)
His team's results with him on the ice minus its results with him on the bench. It removes much of the "good team makes everyone look good" effect. A rel xGA/60 of −0.6 means the team allows 0.6 fewer expected goals per 60 with him on.
Isolated impact
A player's own effect on 5v5 chances, with his linemates, the opposing skaters, zone starts, the score and home ice separated out by a regression over every shift. Often called RAPM elsewhere. See Isolated impact.
CF% / CA
Corsi: all shot attempts (on goal, missed and blocked). A rough possession measure.
HD xGA
Expected goals against from high-danger shots, mostly from the slot and crease.
Zone starts
Where a player's shifts begin on a faceoff. A high own-zone share means harder, defensive deployment.
ixG, ig
Individual expected goals and goals: the player's own shots only.
Goals above expected
Goals minus individual xG. Positive means he finishes better than an average shooter from the same spots.
Percentile
The share of the comparison pool he's better than. 90th means better than 90% of them.
Wins above replacement
Stats-Zor's estimate of how many wins a player adds over a replacement-level call-up. See Value and worth.
Era-adjusted
Scoring rescaled to a common league-wide scoring level, so a 1980s point and a 2020s point can be compared. See Career vs. career.
NHL EDGE
The NHL's puck and player tracking (2021-22 on): skating speed, speed bursts, distance skated, shot speed and zone time.
GSAx
Goals saved above expected, for goalies: the expected goals of every shot he faced minus the goals he allowed. +10 means he stopped 10 more than an average goalie would have on the same shots.
Sv% vs expected
His save % minus the save % an average goalie would post on his shots (1 − xGA ÷ shots on goal), in percentage points.
GSAA
Goals saved above average: shots faced × (his save % − league save %). Works back to 1955-56, before expected goals existed.
Quality start
A start with a save % at or above the league average, or .885+ when he faced 20 shots or fewer. A really bad start is under .850.
Age
Age on February 1 of the season, the same convention Hockey-Reference uses.

Pools and percentiles

Most colors, scores and ranks are percentiles against a pool of players at the same position in the same season. Forwards are compared with forwards, defensemen with defensemen and goalies with goalies, never across positions. Goalie pools are described under Goalie page basics.

  • Regulars (default): at least 12 minutes a game for forwards or 16 for defensemen, and at least 35% of the season's games (minimum 5).
  • Top-pair D / top-six F: at least 20 minutes for defensemen or 16 for forwards. A tougher comparison group.
percentile = (players below him + half of players tied with him) ÷ pool size × 100

For stats where lower is better (xGA/60, giveaways, PK xGA/60 and so on) the percentile is flipped, so a higher percentile always means better. Faceoff percentile is computed among centers with 100+ draws only.

Limits: percentiles say how a player ranks, not by how much. Near the middle of the pack, a small difference in the raw stat can move a percentile a lot.

Profile

The two cards at the top show the season line (goals, assists, points, games), ice time, 5v5 xGF% and relative xGF%, plus three scores from the two-way composite: defense, offense and role difficulty. Trophies and Cups come from the NHL's records. Age, size and handedness come from NHL bios.

Each card is edged in his team's color, with the team's abbreviation and his current jersey number (shown only on a card for the team he plays for now). If he has since moved, the card adds a small line with his current team and number, like "Now #7 · VAN" on Brendan Gallagher's Montreal seasons. There are no team logos or player photos: those belong to the NHL, its teams and photographers.

Box score detail

Under the season scores, each card lists the season's box score in more detail, from the NHL's own stats reports: goals and points at even strength, on the power play and shorthanded; primary and secondary assists; shots on net out of all shot attempts (missed and blocked included); shooting percentage next to what his chances were worth (MoneyPuck's expected goals, so "+4.4 goals vs. expected" means he scored 4.4 more than an average shooter would have on the same shots); penalties drawn and taken; and, for players who take 50+ draws, faceoff win % overall and in his own and the offensive zone.

A last line, for the record, lists game-winning goals, overtime goals, empty-net goals, shootout goals and plus/minus. These are shown as plain counts with no percentiles because they barely repeat from season to season: who scores the winner depends mostly on when goals happen, and plus/minus mixes in linemates, goaltending and luck (the expected-goal share above it tells the same story better). Nothing in this block changes value; the value model already counts goals, on-ice play, the power play and penalties properly.

Big nights counts, from MoneyPuck's game-by-game data: hat tricks (3+ goals in a game), multi-point games (2+ points, with the share of his games) and 4+ point games; his longest point streak, longest goal streak and longest run of games without a point; and, this season, a point or goal streak still running (2+ games). A game he missed doesn't break a streak, the NHL's own convention. The career line adds up every season since 2008-09 (labeled "Since 2008-09" for players who started earlier) and names his best single-season point streak. Checked against the NHL's game logs for 30 player-seasons: games, hat tricks, multi-point games and longest point streak all matched exactly.

Limits: like the rest of the record line, these are mostly luck and never feed value: a long point streak usually rides a hot shooting run. A streak that carries over from one season into the next is counted separately in each.

Tools (NHL EDGE)

For 2021-22 on, each card shows tracking data from the NHL's EDGE system: top skating speed, number of 20+ mph bursts, hardest shot, distance skated per game, and share of 5v5 time spent in the offensive zone. Percentiles are the NHL's own, forwards against forwards and defensemen against defensemen.

Trend: his top speed and 20+ mph bursts per game this season against his best earlier EDGE season (20+ games). It's flagged Slowing if top speed is down 0.5 mph or more, or bursts per game are down 25% or more. Speed usually fades before production does, so it's an early sign for older players.

Why it's not in the value model: speed and shot power describe how a player plays, not how much he helps. Fast players can drive play poorly and slow ones can be excellent, and what tools produce (chances, goals, suppression) is already counted. EDGE is used for style instead: in the stat board's "most similar" match and in the trend.

Limits: top speed and hardest shot are single peak moments from one game. Bursts and distance are season totals, so they depend on games and minutes played. No EDGE data exists before 2021-22.

Part one · how he played

Two-way composite

Three scores, each an average of percentiles, then combined with the weight sliders.

Defensemen Defense = 75% × avg(xGA/60, rel xGA/60, HD xGA/60, CA/60, PK xGA/60) + 25% × avg(blocks/60, takeaways/60, DZ giveaways/60 [lower better], hits/60) Offense = avg(xGF/60, rel xGF/60, ixG/60, 5v5 points/60, PP points/60) Role = avg(TOI, PK TOI, own-zone start %, team xGF% without him [lower = harder]) Forwards Defense = 80% × avg(xGA/60, rel xGA/60, HD xGA/60, PK xGA/60) + 20% × avg(takeaways/60, giveaways/60 [lower better]) Offense = avg(goals/60, 5v5 points/60, ixG/60, xGF/60, rel xGF/60, PP points/60) Role = avg(TOI, PK TOI, own-zone start %, team xGF% without him, faceoff % [centers only]) Composite = (Defense × wD + Offense × wO + Role × wR) ÷ (wD + wO + wR)

Why: raw points reward offense only. This puts suppression, deployment and teammate context on equal footing, and the sliders let you decide how much each matters. "Role difficulty" credits players who carry hard minutes on weak teams. A missing piece (no PK time, say) is skipped, not counted as zero.

Limits: hits, blocks, takeaways and giveaways are recorded by arena scorers and vary by building, so they get a small weight. On-ice stats are shared with linemates.

Stat board

A grid of raw values, each colored by its percentile in the pool: blue is good, orange is poor. On role columns, blue means a harder job. Two ways to fill it:

  • Top-ranked: the highest composite scores at his position.
  • Most similar: the players whose percentile profile across every board column is closest to his.
match % = 100 − √(average of (his percentile − their percentile)² across columns) (players missing more than 40% of the columns are skipped) when both players have NHL EDGE data, top speed, 20+ mph bursts and shot speed percentiles are added as style columns at half weight each

Your comparison player and anyone you add always stay on the board.

Head to head

A radar of eight percentiles, each player against his own position group. Defensemen: ice time, own-zone starts, xGA/60, rel xGA/60, PK xGA/60, blocks + takeaways (average of the two percentiles), rel xGF/60 and 5v5 points/60. Forwards: ice time, goals/60, points/60, ixG/60, rel xGF/60, rel xGA/60, PK xGA/60, and faceoffs for centers or takeaways for wingers. Lower-is-better stats are flipped, so farther out is always better. The table beside it shows raw values, with a dot on the better one.

Shot environment and deployment

  • Left chart: 5v5 xGF/60 against xGA/60 for every regular at the position. Gray lines mark the medians. Up and left means more chances for and fewer against.
  • Right chart: own-zone start share against relative xGF%. Right means tougher starts; up means the team is better with him on the ice.

Isolated impact

Relative numbers compare the team with him and without him, so they're thrown off by who he plays with and how good the team is without him. A great defenseman on a deep team (Cale Makar in 2025-26) can look ordinary because Colorado stayed good when he sat. Isolated impact asks a different question: holding everyone else on the ice constant, how many more chances happen per 60 when he's out there?

For every stretch of 5v5 play with the same ten skaters on the ice (a "stint"), from 2010-11 on: attacking team's xG per 60 = league base + home ice + zone of the opening faceoff + score state + each attacker's offense + each defender's defense Each stint counts twice (once per team attacking), weighted by its length. Solved by ridge regression: every skater is pulled toward average (0) by a penalty worth 20 hours of ice time, so a player needs a real sample to move far from 0.
  • Who was on the ice: NHL shift charts, second by second. Goalies are removed; only stints with both goalies in net and five skaters a side count.
  • Chances: MoneyPuck's expected goals for each unblocked shot, matched to the stint by game, period and time. Empty-net shots and the playoffs are left out.
  • Zone and score: from the NHL play-by-play. A stint that starts with an offensive-zone faceoff produces more chances for that team in the first seconds, so that's taken out. Teams that are trailing push and teams that are leading sit back, so score state is taken out too.
  • The penalty: 20 hours was the best of 5 to 120 when 20% of 2024-25 games were held out and predicted from the rest.
  • One season or three: the three-season fit pools that season and the two before it. It's steadier, and it's what most public models show.
  • Where each player starts (one-season fit): instead of pulling everyone toward 0, each player is pulled toward 75% of his estimate from the season before (when he played 200+ five-on-five minutes then). Players with no usable last season (mostly rookies) start from a box-score estimate: what his individual numbers that season (expected goals, goals, primary assists, shot attempts, high-danger chances, takeaways, giveaways, blocks, hits, penalties drawn and taken, all per 60) usually mean for isolated impact at his position, learned from earlier seasons. Each season then moves him from that starting point only as far as the new evidence says. It predicts next season about 10% better than starting everyone at 0 (0.342 vs. 0.312 over 15 pairs of seasons; best of 8 versions tested: 0%, 50%, 75% or 100% of last season, each with and without the box-score start). The three-season fit still starts at 0.

Offense is xGF/60 he adds (positive is good). Defense is xGA/60 he adds (negative is good: fewer chances against). Net is offense minus defense. Percentiles are among regulars at his position. Because of the penalty, isolated numbers are smaller than relative ones, about half the spread. Compare players by percentile, not by size.

Which predicts better? For every skater with 500+ 5v5 minutes in back-to-back seasons, this season's number is lined up against next season's two ways: next season's on-ice xG differential and next season's relative differential. Using both keeps the test from favouring either method. Over 15 pairs of seasons (2010-11 to 2025-26), each method predicts its own kind of number best: isolated alone lines up better with next season's on-ice results (0.35 vs. 0.28 for relative), and relative alone lines up better with next season's relative results (0.32 vs. 0.27). Scored on both at once, the best mix is about two-thirds isolated and one-third relative (0.319, against 0.300 for relative alone and 0.312 for isolated alone), so that's the mix the value model uses. The skater page shows the full table. The test reruns every night as seasons are added.

Same method, other measures. Each player card also shows the regression run on other targets:

  • Goals and shot attempts at 5v5: the same fit with actual goals or unblocked shot attempts (not expected goals) per 60. Goals are the noisiest; shot attempts the steadiest. They're for comparison only and don't feed value.
  • Power play and penalty kill: a separate fit over every 5-on-4 stretch. Each row is the power-play team's expected goals per 60, with a column for each power-play skater (what he adds) and each penalty killer (what he allows), plus home ice and the opening faceoff zone. The penalty is 80 hours, the best of 5 to 80 at predicting next season. Shown from 50+ minutes (150+ over three seasons).
Limits
  • It starts in 2010-11, the first season with NHL shift charts. A few games each season have no shift chart and are skipped (57 of 1,312 in 2024-25).
  • The regression separates linemates and opponents only as far as they play apart. Two players who are almost always together (the Sedins, a fixed top pair) share credit roughly evenly.
  • It doesn't adjust for scorekeeper differences between rinks, or for the quality of the goalie behind him (only chances are measured, not goals).
  • Early in a season the single-season numbers sit close to where the player started (his last season, or his box-score estimate). That's the starting point doing its job, not a sign nothing has changed.
  • Penalty-kill numbers barely repeat from one season to the next (a 0.14 correlation even for on-ice numbers), so read them loosely. Power-play units are fixed groups, so credit inside a unit is split roughly evenly.

Scouting lists (sidebar)

The sidebar on the skater and goalie pages (a tap-to-open line on phones and smaller laptops) holds scouting lists for the selected season, eleven tabs on the skater page and seven on the goalie page. Click a name to open his page. The top 10 show first and "Show more" lists everyone who qualifies; skater lists can be filtered to forwards or defense. The big number next to each name is a percentile among regulars at the same position that season (97th = better than 97% of them; for goalies, among regular goalies), except in Due for a bounce, where it's goals (skaters) or save % points (goalies) compared with expected. Each list says which under its description. To avoid small samples, every list built on three-season isolated impact needs 1,500+ five-on-five minutes over those three seasons (about two seasons of regular play).

  • Doing more than the box score: isolated impact (net, three seasons) in the top 20%, points per game not in the top 10%, and at least one of low points (bottom half per game), weak linemates (bottom third in linemates' quality) or tough minutes (top fifth in opponents' quality). Sorted by the gap between isolated impact and points.
  • Carrying the load: the flip side: players who do score but carry a weak team. Isolated impact in the top 20%, points per game in the top 40%, and either his team's 5v5 expected-goal share without him on the ice in the bottom third or linemates in the bottom third. Sorted with the most elite, most productive and least helped first. It can't prove he'd do even more on a better team; the case is that his own impact is elite and the team falls apart when he sits (the Linemates section shows his partners with and without him).
  • Shutdown: isolated defense (expected goals against, three seasons) in the top 20%, while facing real competition: top 40% in opponents' quality or in time against top lines. Without that second part the list fills with sheltered depth players who suppress chances against weaker opponents. Penalty-kill time is shown when it's 1:30 a game or more.
  • Breakout watch: age 24 or under, single-season isolated impact in the top quarter (800+ five-on-five minutes) and up at least 0.15 xG/60 from the season before (500+ minutes then). Sorted by the size of the jump.
  • Due for a bounce: scored at least 4 goals below expected this season (scaled to games played in a season still going), after finishing at least 2 goals above expected over the two seasons before with 40+ games in each. Finishing swings a lot from year to year, so a proven finisher in a slump usually recovers. Sorted by how far below expected.
  • Big minutes, low impact: ice time in the top quarter at his position, but three-season isolated impact in the bottom 20%. This isn't a verdict on his contract (the site has no contract data); add his cap hit on his page to judge that.
  • Faceoffs: four lists over three seasons (players who played this season), from the NHL's faceoff report by zone. Best: highest win % with 1,500+ draws. Own zone: highest win % in his defensive zone with 300+ draws there, the centers trusted after an icing or on the penalty kill. Struggles: lowest own-zone win % with the same minimum. Offensive: highest win % in the other team's zone with 300+ draws there. Faceoff % is a real, repeatable skill, but a single win is worth only about a hundredth of a goal (a bit more in the defensive zone), so the tab describes the skill and doesn't change value. Zone counts start in 2009-10.
  • Special teams: three lists over three seasons (players who played this season), from the NHL's even-strength, power-play and shorthanded points and MoneyPuck's ice time and shorthanded expected goals. Each is ranked within his position. Power-play heavy: 100+ points, top 20% at his position in share of points scored on the power play, with a chip comparing his five-on-five scoring to every regular at his position (defensemen who quarterback a power play naturally score a big share there). 5v5 scorers, little PP: 2,000+ even-strength minutes, top 30% at his position in even-strength points per 60, and under 1:30 of power-play time a night: players who might produce more if given power-play minutes. PP struggles, Penalty kill and PK struggles compare his on-ice results with his own teammates on the same special team, so a team's strong or weak power play or penalty kill doesn't decide who appears (raw numbers would fill the lists with whole units, like Florida's power play or Detroit's penalty kill). Each season, his expected goals for per 60 on the power play (or against per 60 on the penalty kill) is set against the time-weighted rate of the team's other players on that unit; the three seasons are combined by his minutes. PP struggles: 300+ power-play minutes, bottom quarter at his position. Penalty kill and PK struggles: 300+ shorthanded minutes, top and bottom quarter at his position. The chip shows his rate next to his teammates'. These still mix in his unit-mates and his role (a PP2 player is compared with PP1 teammates, too), and special-teams results barely repeat from season to season (it's why the value model gives isolated penalty-kill numbers no weight), so read them as leads to look into, not ratings. The 5v5, power-play and penalty-kill lists show a percentile as the big number, like the other tabs; power-play heavy shows the share of points. Nothing in the tab changes value.
  • Puck play: five lists over three seasons, all situations (players who played this season, 1,500+ minutes), each ranked within his position. Takes it away: most takeaways per 60. Careful with it and Loose with it: takes the puck away about as often as he gives it away, or gives it away far more often than he takes it (ranked by his share of takeaways among his takeaways and giveaways, 60+ of them). Chips show how many giveaways for every takeaway, then where his giveaways, takeaways and points per 60 sit at his position, so you can tell whether it comes from many giveaways (often players who carry the puck), few takeaways (often stay-at-home defensemen), or both. Shot blockers: most blocked shots per 60. Physical: most hits per 60. These are counted by each arena's own scorekeeper, and arenas differ a lot: in a Nov 2026 test over eight seasons, through 2023-24 the stingiest arena recorded about half the league rate of takeaways and giveaways and the most generous 1.6 to 1.8 times, and the same arenas were high or low every year (since 2024-25 they sit within about 10-15% of the league). So every game's counts are divided by that arena's factor for the season (its rate, both teams, over the league rate, pulled toward 1 when it has few home games). In the same test, a player's hits and blocks per 60 repeated very strongly from one season to the next (correlation about 0.9), takeaways and giveaways moderately (about 0.6), and giveaways were only weakly tied to scoring, so the "loose" list isn't just the stars who carry the puck. Blocking and hitting often mean the other team has the puck, so these lists describe a style, not value. Nothing in the tab changes value.
  • Discipline: four lists over three seasons (players who played this season), from the NHL's penalty records and the play-by-play of every game since 2008-09. Penalties drawn and taken count minor penalties only and leave out scrum calls (roughing, fighting, unsportsmanlike conduct, misconducts), which usually go both ways and rarely change the man advantage; with them in, the "draws penalties" list filled up with scrum players. Draws penalties: most minors drawn per 60 (1,500+ minutes). Undisciplined: most minors taken per 60, mostly stick and obstruction penalties. Clean physical: hits in the top 30% at his position (arena-adjusted, as in Puck play) while taking fewer minors than half his position. Fighters: fighting majors (60+ games, 3+ fights), shown as a count and per 82 games, with instigator penalties, misconducts (any reason, not only fights) and roughing minors. Who won a fight isn't recorded anywhere official, so it isn't shown, and nothing measures whether a fight helped teammates. A Nov 2026 test over ten seasons found these repeat from one season to the next: minors drawn per 60 (correlation about 0.64), minors taken (about 0.57), fights per game (about 0.74), roughing (about 0.53); misconducts much less (about 0.34). Drawing and taking penalties already count in value through the power play; this tab changes nothing.
  • Leaders: plain five-on-five leaderboards, best or worst, for this season or three seasons (players who played this season). 5v5 xG share: the share of expected goals (both teams) that went his team's way while he was on the ice, with a chip comparing it with his teammates when he was off the ice (raw shares lean heavily on the team). Creates: expected goals from his own shots per 60. Suppresses: fewest expected goals against per 60 while he's on (worst = most), with a chip against his teammates. Forwards and defensemen are listed separately. This season needs 600+ five-on-five minutes, scaled to the share of the season played (never under 150); three seasons need 1,500+. Forward lines and D pairs: each combination's expected-goal share together, from MoneyPuck's lines data, with a chip comparing it with the team's own share that season; 300+ minutes together this season (scaled, never under 100) or 400+ over three seasons (combinations that played together this season). Click a line or pair to open the first player with the second as his comparison. Most combinations don't last long enough to qualify, so these lists are short early in a season.
  • Goalies get seven tabs. One goalie season is about half luck, so every goalie list needs at least 1,000 shots this season (scaled to the share of the season played), and three-season judgments need 2,500+ shots.
    • Little help: save % above expected in the top 20% of regulars, with a losing record (bottom 40% in points percentage), tough shots (top fifth in expected goals per shot faced) or busy nights (top fifth in shots faced per 60).
    • Stealing games: save % above expected in the top 20% and a record in the top half, on a team that gives up lots of expected goals per 60 or dangerous shots (top third). The goalie is the reason they win.
    • Danger specialist: save % above expected on high-danger shots (from the slot and in tight) in the top 20%, over three seasons with 300+ high-danger shots, and a regular this season.
    • Breakout goalie: age 27 or under (goalies develop later than skaters), save % above expected in the top third and up 0.8+ points on the season before (500+ shots then).
    • Due for a bounce: save % at least 0.5 points below expected this season, after 0.3+ above expected over the two seasons before (800+ shots in each).
    • Heavy workload, below average: starts in the top quarter, but save % above expected in the bottom 20% over three seasons. Not a verdict on his contract.
    • Leaders: best or worst goals saved above expected (expected goals on the shots he faced minus goals allowed), this season or three seasons. High danger counts high-danger shots only and compares him with the league-average goalie on those shots that season, because MoneyPuck's high-danger expected goals run about 30% above what goalies actually allow (every goalie would look "above expected" otherwise).
Limits
  • The Leaders tab is a plain leaderboard and describes only; it changes nothing in value. Early in a season it rests on few games, which is why its minimums grow with the season.
  • These are starting points for a closer look, not verdicts. Open the player's page and check the details.
  • Players whose points are already in the top 10% are left out of the box-score list on purpose (Moritz Seider in 2025-26); he shows up under Carrying the load and Breakout watch instead.
  • Breakout watch and Due for a bounce lean on one season of results, with minute and game minimums to keep the samples honest. Early in a season some lists are short or empty until players reach the minimums.
  • Skater lists built on isolated impact start in 2010-11, when NHL shift charts begin. Goalie lists swing more than skater lists even with the shot minimums, and some are short (two or three names) in a given season.

Shot maps

Unblocked shot attempts (on goal, missed and goals; MoneyPuck's shot file doesn't include blocked shots) in the offensive zone, in 5 × 5 ft cells. Regular season only; empty-net shots are left out.

  • Where he shoots: his share of attempts from each spot minus the league share for his position. Rings mark goals.
  • Finishing: goals minus expected goals. Smoothed, it's per attempt (full color at ±0.06 goals per attempt, faded where he rarely shoots); in cells, it's the total per cell with at least 3 attempts (full color at ±2.5 goals).
  • Smoothed (default) or cells: smoothed blends each spot with its neighbours, weighting cells by a bell curve about 7 ft wide (a Gaussian with a 7 ft standard deviation), so one lucky night from an odd spot doesn't paint a whole cell. It's drawn every 1.25 ft and fades where few shots come from. Full color for where he shoots is 0.6 percentage points per cell. Cells shows the raw 5 × 5 ft counts (full color at 1.2 points). Hover a cell for its numbers in either view.
  • Slot share: attempts from 20 ft in front of the net out to the goal line, within 10 ft of center.

Situations: all, 5v5 (5 skaters a side), or power play (his team has more skaters).

Limits: cells with few shots are noisy, especially in finishing view. Coordinates are MoneyPuck's arena-adjusted locations.

Linemates and partners

Built from MoneyPuck's 5v5 line and pair data: forward trios for forwards, defense pairs for defensemen.

together = sum of every line/pair containing both players him without partner = his 5v5 totals − together partner without him = partner's 5v5 totals − together

Teammates with at least 30 minutes together are shown. xGF% is left blank under 10 minutes.

Limits: "without" is everything else he played, mostly with other partners, so it isn't a clean test. Forward-and-defense combinations aren't available in the data.

Team swap

Projects a player's 5v5 impact onto another team in place of one of its players.

his impact = his on-ice rate − his team's rate with him on the bench (xGF/60 and xGA/60) him on new team = that team's rates without the replaced player + his impact xG differential = (xGF/60 − xGA/60) × his 5v5 minutes per game × 82 ÷ 60 wins ≈ xG differential ÷ 6

Limits: it assumes a player's impact moves with him unchanged. New partners, coaches, systems and roles all change that in reality. Treat it as the direction and rough size of the change.

Part two · how he's used

Role and fit

Slots, every game: from MoneyPuck's game-by-game data, each team's players are ranked by 5v5 ice time that night.

  • Even strength: forwards 1–3 = 1st line, 4–6 = 2nd, 7–9 = 3rd, 10+ = 4th. Defensemen 1–2 = 1st pair, 3–4 = 2nd, 5+ = 3rd.
  • Power play: the top 5 skaters by 5-on-4 time are PP1, the next 5 are PP2 (30+ seconds needed).
  • Penalty kill: within forwards and defensemen separately, the top 2 by 4-on-5 time are PK1 and the next 2 are PK2.
  • Zone nights: 2+ more offensive- than defensive-zone starts makes an offensive-zone-heavy night, the reverse a defensive-zone-heavy night, otherwise balanced.

Season role labels: his average slot across games. PP1 means PP1 in at least half his games; PP2 means a PP unit in at least half. Spot PP means at least 15% of games. Same for PK.

Role averages: the league's combined 5v5 results for everyone in each slot. Every "Role avg" in the tables comes from these.

Three ways to judge fit:

5v5 results = weighted percentile of forwards: xGF% 35%, rel xGF% 25%, 5v5 points/60 40% defensemen: xGF% 35%, rel xGF% 25%, rel xGA/60 25%, 5v5 points/60 15% Overall value = percentile of wins above replacement per 82 games (Part 3 model) Both are blended over up to 3 seasons: weights 3, 2, 1 (most recent first) × games played. fits slot = slots + 0.5 − slots × percentile ÷ 100 (4 slots for forwards, 3 for D) → top quarter of forwards ≈ 1st line ... bottom quarter ≈ 4th line Against his role = his 5v5 xGF% − league xGF% for the slots he played, weighted by his minutes in each

Flags:

  • Even strength and overall: his average slot vs. his fit slot. A gap of 0.6 slot or more either way is flagged.
  • Against his role: ±1.5 points of xGF% or more is flagged.
  • Power play: needs 0.75+ PP minutes a game. PP points/60 in the 70th percentile or higher is PP1 production, 40th–70th is PP2. Unused players in the 70th percentile or higher for 5v5 scoring are "worth a PP look."
  • Penalty kill: needs 0.75+ PK minutes a game. PK xGA/60 in the 65th percentile or higher is PK1 results, 40th–65th is PK2. Unused players in the 70th percentile or higher for rel xGA/60 are "PK candidates."
  • Zone starts: "Can take own-zone starts" needs 50+ minutes on defensive-zone-heavy nights, a defense score of 60+, and xGF% no more than 4 points worse than on offensive-zone nights. "Best sheltered" means an offense score of 60+ with a defense score under 45.

Why three views: a power-play specialist or elite finisher can read low at 5v5 but high overall. A first-liner facing top competition can read low on both but hold up against his role. Reading all three together avoids one-number verdicts.

Limits:

  • Competition: who a player was on the ice against isn't in the public data, which biases 5v5 numbers against first-liners. "Against his role" is the best available correction.
  • Cause and effect: coaches move players partly because of form, injuries and matchups, and promotion usually brings better linemates.
  • Small samples: under 30 minutes is left blank, and one season is noisy, hence the three-season blend.

Who he played against and with

Built from the same NHL shift charts as isolated impact, over the same 5v5 stretches (five skaters a side, both goalies in), 2010-11 on.

  • Against the opponent's top line: share of his 5v5 time with two or more of the other team's three most-used forwards that night on the ice.
  • Against the opponent's top pair: share of his 5v5 time with both of the other team's two most-used defensemen on the ice.
  • Opponents' quality: the average isolated impact (net xG/60, three-season fit where there is one) of the five opponents on the ice with him, weighted by time.
  • Linemates' quality: the same for the four teammates on the ice with him.

Ranks are among regulars at his position (hardest first for competition, best first for linemates). The short summary calls the top fifth "some of the toughest" or "some of the best" and the bottom fifth "sheltered" or "weak".

Limits
  • Competition varies much less than linemates. In 2025-26 the toughest and easiest regular defensemen differed by only about 0.04 xG/60 in opponents' quality, against about 0.3 for linemates. Being matched against top lines is real, but the bigger effect on a player's numbers is usually who he plays with.
  • "Most-used" is by 5v5 time that game, so injuries, penalties and blowouts can shuffle who counts as a top line on a given night.
  • Quality uses isolated impact, which has its own noise, especially for players with few minutes.

Team deployment card

Every regular on a team with at least 25% of the season's games. It shows his average slot ("used as"), 5v5 fit, overall fit and vs. role, with power-play and penalty-kill use and fit, and zone fit, all as defined above. The even-strength flag here uses overall fit. Traded players show their whole season.

Part three · what he's worth

Value and worth

Stats-Zor's own wins-above-replacement model. It's simpler than commercial models like Evolving-Hockey's GAR, but every piece is visible.

Goals above replacement (per season) 5v5 offense = (5v5 offense rate − replacement) × 5v5 hours 5v5 defense = (replacement − 5v5 defense rate) × 5v5 hours Finishing = his finishing per shot over 3 seasons × kept share × his shots this season (finishing = goals − individual xG, all situations; kept share = 3-season shots ÷ (shots + 400) for forwards, ÷ (shots + 2,000) for defensemen) Power play = (PP rate − replacement) × PP hours Penalty kill = 0.25 × (replacement − on-ice PK xGA/60) × PK hours Penalties = 0.15 × (penalties drawn − penalties taken) 5v5 rate = 67% × isolated impact (stretched) + 33% × 0.5 × relative xGF/60 or xGA/60 PP rate = 50% × isolated PP impact (stretched) + 50% × 0.3 × on-ice PP xGF/60 Replacement = 20th percentile among regulars at the position (80th for the against stats), roughly what a call-up provides. Wins = goals ÷ 6

Why these weights: on-ice stats are shared by five skaters, so relative 5v5 and special-teams credit is scaled down. Finishing is counted as skill, not as whatever happened: see below. A penalty drawn is worth about 0.15 goals, roughly the value of a power play.

Finishing (tested, Nov 2026): beating expected goals is part skill and part luck. Over every season since 2008, a forward kept about 28% of one season's finishing edge into the next (39% with 200+ shots) and 53% of a three-season edge; a defenseman kept about 7% of one season's and 15% of three seasons'. The share that carries over follows shots ÷ (shots + 400) for forwards and shots ÷ (shots + 2,000) for defensemen almost exactly. So finishing credit is his finishing per shot over this season and the two before, times that share, times his shots this season. It predicts next season's finishing 11% better than the old rule (half of this season's goals above expected) for forwards and 14% better for defensemen. Proven shooters (Pastrňák, Matthews) keep credit through a cold year; a one-season hot streak counts less.

Offensive defensemen: a defenseman's goals above expected come mostly from long shots through traffic, deflections and screens, and barely repeat from year to year, so most of it is treated as bounces. A defenseman with a big scoring year (Cale Makar in 2024-25: 9.7 goals above expected) gets much less finishing credit than his goal total suggests. His chance creation (5v5 offense and power play) counts in full. When the gap is 6+ goals over three seasons, his Value card says so, and every card has a read-only Finishing method box comparing what happened, the old half rule and the rule used.

The power-play blend: power-play credit mixes his isolated 5-on-4 impact with his on-ice numbers, half and half. The isolated number measures him against an average power-play skater, while the on-ice number is a level, so the isolated number is first stretched to the same spread as 0.3 × on-ice and re-centred on the average on-ice level at his position. That keeps the league's total power-play credit about the same and only moves it between teammates. That mix predicted next season's power-play results best (0.394 vs. 0.389 for on-ice alone). It takes some credit away from players on a great power-play unit who aren't driving it, and gives it to the ones who are. The penalty kill stays on on-ice numbers: the isolated version predicted worse. Players with under 50 power-play minutes use on-ice only.

The 5v5 blend: the 5v5 rates mix his isolated impact with his relative numbers. The mix is 67% isolated, the best of the mixes tried in the forecast test. Isolated numbers come out about half as spread as relative ones, so each season they're stretched (by position) to the same spread as 0.5 × relative before mixing. That keeps 5v5 play's share of all value where it was, and only changes which players get the credit. Seasons before 2010-11 have no shift charts and use relative alone. The Value card's 5v5 method box shows his 5v5 goals three ways (relative only, isolated only, the blend). It's for reading only: worth, contracts, projections and the trade machine all use the blend.

Price of a win = 32 teams × (cap × 0.88 skater share − 20 skaters × minimum salary) ÷ all positive wins produced that season Worth = minimum salary + max(0, wins per 82 games) × price of a win % of cap = worth ÷ that season's cap Worth over last 3 seasons = minimum salary + max(0, blended wins per 82) × this season's price of a win (that season and the two before, weights 3, 2, 1 × games; needs 2+ seasons)

This season vs. last 3 seasons: the Value card shows both. One season can swing a lot (Cale Makar's 2025-26 comes out at about $9.7M against $10.5M over three seasons), so the three-season figure is the steadier read on what a player is worth. It's the same blend the fair-value contract starts from, priced at this season's cap. If you enter a cap hit, it's compared with both.

Caps used ($M): 2008-09 56.7 · 09-10 56.8 · 10-11 59.4 · 11-12 64.3 · 12-13 60.0 (lockout) · 13-14 64.3 · 14-15 69.0 · 15-16 71.4 · 16-17 73.0 · 17-18 75.0 · 18-19 79.5 · 19-20 to 21-22 81.5 · 22-23 82.5 · 23-24 83.5 · 24-25 88.0 · 25-26 95.5 · 26-27 104.0 · 27-28 113.5 (projected), then an assumed +5% a year. Minimum salary follows each season's CBA figure, rising to $1M by 2029-30.

Limits: stars come out "worth" far more than their pay. That's normal for value models, because the max salary, entry-level and RFA contracts keep the best players underpaid, so read the dollars as relative value. A partial current season uses each player's pace over 82 games, so early-season figures swing. Goalies have their own version, under Goalie value.

Fair-value contract

Starting point = his wins per 82, blended over 3 seasons (weights 3, 2, 1 × games) If those seasons add up to fewer than 150 games: starting point = average regular + (his blend − average regular) × games ÷ 150 (average regular = games-weighted wins per 82 of players with 40+ games at his position, last complete season) Each future year: last year's wins × the age factor for the age he's leaving (measured from our own data each night; forwards and defensemen separately, see below) Worth that year = minimum salary + wins × price of a win (scaled to that year's cap) Fair AAV for an N-year deal = average worth over those N years

Terms run 1–7 years, from next season. The current CBA allows 7 years to re-sign with your own team and 6 as a free agent.

The age curve comes from the data. For every skater since 2008-09 who was a regular worth 1+ wins per 82 the season before, it compares his wins per 82 one season with the next, by age, weighting by games. Picking players by the season before keeps one lucky year from looking like aging. Players who leave the NHL count as dropping to replacement level, so the curve isn't flattered by only counting the ones who lasted. An age group needs at least 100 players, or it's merged with the next one, and the curve can't rise again after it starts falling. As of November 2026:

Forwards peak around 23–24 and lose a little each year after (about 3% a year at 24–26), more from about 27; defensemen hold steady to about 30 and then drop quickly. This measures value, mostly 5v5 play, not points: a veteran can keep his points with power-play time while his 5v5 impact fades, and individual players vary a lot around the average. Tested by projecting every player from 2010-11 on and checking what he actually did, this curve had about 1%, 2% and 3% less error one, two and three years out than the hand-set curve it replaced (+10% a year to 23, flat 27–28, then −5% to −15%), with the biggest gains for forwards and young players. Stars don't age better: elite forwards lose about as many wins a year as everyone else, which is a smaller share of their value, and a percentage curve already gives them that. The fitted numbers are in value.json (age_curve) and can shift a little as seasons are added. Goalies keep their own hand-set curve, below, because there are too few of them to measure one.

Small samples: a hot 20-game start used to project like an established star (Tristan Luneau, with 28 NHL games, projected as the third-best defenseman). Now a projection built on fewer than 150 games keeps only its share of the gap to an average regular, so 28 games keeps 19% of it. Players with 150+ games in the window aren't touched. Tested on every season from 2011-12 on (does the blend predict next season?): this cut the error for small-sample players by about a quarter. Pulling everyone toward average predicted slightly better still, but it would have marked down established stars by about 15%, so it isn't used. The card says when a player's projection was pulled.

Limits: this is what his projected value would justify, not a prediction of what he'll sign for. RFA rights, arbitration, the max-salary cap, cap crunches and market quirks all move real contracts. The model also uses one average price per win, so it can't show the premium teams pay for true stars: in 2026 Cale Makar signed for $20.4M a year and Leo Carlsson for $18M (about 16–18% of the cap), while the model's fair value for top players sits around 12–15%.

Trade machine

  • Players: his blended wins per 82 × the share of this season left, plus the projected wins for each season left on his contract after this one, plus any restricted-free-agent seasons his team still controls (below). The site doesn't know contracts, so you enter the years left (0 for a rental); until you do, only the rest of this season counts (plus any RFA seasons) and the verdict flags it. Worth is priced the same way. Goalies are on the rosters too, valued with the goalie model in wins per season at their own workload, and priced at the goalie price of a win.
  • Rosters: each team's current NHL roster, plus players who played in the last two seasons and aren't on any active roster but whose NHL player record still lists that team (sitting out, unsigned restricted free agents, long-term injury). Those are marked "not on active roster". Unsigned unrestricted free agents don't belong to a team, so they aren't listed. A player who's sitting out or suspended is still valued as if he plays (and is paid) the rest of this season, which is right once he's traded but not while the standoff lasts.
  • Team control (RFA): a player can't become an unrestricted free agent until he's 27 (as of June 30 when his deal ends) or has 7 accrued seasons. Until then his team keeps his rights. The site estimates this from his birth date and NHL games, counting an accrued season as one with 40+ NHL games (25+ for goalies). The "Team keeps his rights after this deal (RFA)" box is checked automatically when he'd still be restricted at the end of his contract, and shows how many seasons it adds. You can uncheck it, or check it again; that choice sticks for that player until the page is reloaded.
  • Cap hits: if you enter one, cost = cap hit × (share of this season left + years left after this one), plus his projected worth for any RFA seasons (their salary isn't known, so they're assumed paid fair value: they add wins but no surplus), and surplus = worth − cost. All dollar figures are totals over the seasons counted, not per year. Each player shows his real salary (cap hit × years) separately from the assumed RFA pay, with a per-year figure for the RFA seasons. The cap-change line counts real salary only. Once every player has a cap hit, the verdict switches from value to surplus.
  • Team plans: each side can be set to a plan that weighs wins by when they arrive. It describes what the team cares about, not where it sits in the standings. Each team weighs what it gets and gives on its own scale, so both can come out ahead. The value and surplus verdicts always use No preference; the other plans add a "Wins by each team's plans" row.

  • Prospects: drafted in the last five drafts, under 100 NHL games, and not on an NHL roster. They get the value of their draft slot, reduced 15% for each full year since the draft. They're listed under their drafting team (Arizona picks under Utah).

"About even" if the gap is under 0.4 wins ($1.5M in surplus mode) or under 8% of everything traded. Cap hits are in millions; a number typed in dollars (8700000) is converted (8.7).

Limits: who actually owns which picks isn't in the free data, so picks are generic. Cap space, retained salary, trade clauses and team needs aren't checked. Contracts aren't included; cap hits you type stay in your browser. The RFA estimate simplifies the CBA (the real accrued-season rule depends on age at signing, and offer sheets, arbitration and waivers aren't modeled), and prospects' college, junior and AHL play isn't in the free data, so a prospect without NHL games is valued only by where he was drafted.

Part four · vs. history

Career vs. career

League scoring rate = all goals ÷ (team games ÷ 2), from NHL team totals each season Era-adjusted points = points × 6.0 ÷ that season's league scoring rate Per 82 = adjusted points ÷ games × 82

6.0 goals a game is a neutral baseline. Since the 1940s, league scoring has ranged from about 4.8 goals a game (early 1950s) to 8.0 (early 1980s). "Same age" freezes both players at the main player's latest completed age. "Same career stage" uses his number of full seasons (40%+ of the schedule played). Ice time only exists from 1997-98 on, and expected-goal stats from 2008-09 on.

Limits: era adjustment doesn't account for roster size, schedule differences or how assists were awarded in different eras. Trophies are winners only; Norris and Hart voting finishes aren't in the NHL's public data.

Career arcs and comps

The arcs chart plots every season by age, for any measure in the buttons (era-adjusted points or goals per 82, ice time, games, or relative xGF%/xGA/60 for 2008+).

Comps: players at the same position with enough games (100, or half of his total, whichever is more) by his current age, compared through that age.

features (weight): era-adj points/82 (1), era-adj goals/82 (0.6), ice time (1, if both have it for 60%+ of games), rel xGF% and rel xGA/60 (1 each, if both have 100+ games of 2008+ data) each feature standardized by its spread across candidates distance = √(weighted average of squared differences); closest = smallest distance

Limits: it compares production and on-ice impact, not size, style or role. Older comps are matched on scoring only.

Teams · teams.html

Teams page

The teams page looks at one team in one season: how it used its players game by game, how its five-on-five play trended, and whether each regular's role matches his results. Pick a team, a season (2008-09 on) and a recent window (10 or 20 games). Changing the season keeps the same franchise (Atlanta becomes Winnipeg, Arizona becomes Utah).

The card at the top shows the season's 5v5 expected-goal share and its rank among all teams, the same over the recent window, the 5v5 goal share, how many skaters dressed, a count of the usage flags from the table below, and the head coach. Head coaches come from the NHL's game-center data, game by game; a change is shown when a new name runs three or more games (a one-night fill-in is ignored). Older seasons are looked up a few at a time, so a season can briefly say it hasn't been looked up yet.

Season trend

The team's share of five-on-five expected goals over its last 10 (or 20) games, drawn after every game. Until the window is full (the first 9 or 19 games), it reaches back into the end of the same franchise's previous season, drawn lighter over a shaded band, so the line starts at game 1; summer roster changes mean those early points mix two different teams. With no previous season (2008-09, or an expansion team's first year), the lighter line is the season so far, from game 3. A dotted line does the same with actual goals, and a dashed line marks the season average. A coaching change is marked where it happened.

team 5v5 xGF in a game = (sum of its skaters' on-ice 5v5 xGF) ÷ 5 (exact: five skaters are always on the ice at 5v5) rolling share at game n = xGF over the last N games ÷ (xGF + xGA over the same games)
What this can and can't see
  • Ten games is a small sample: a hot goalie, a soft stretch of schedule or a few injuries can move it several points. Use 20 games or the season average for anything firmer.
  • Goals swing more than expected goals with shooting and goaltending luck, which is why they're drawn lighter.
  • It is five-on-five only; special teams aren't in it.

Lineup over time

One row per skater who dressed for the team, one square per game. Each night, the team's forwards are ranked by five-on-five ice time into lines of three and its defensemen into pairs of two, the same rule as the role and fit section; darker means higher in the lineup. A blank square means he wasn't in the lineup for this team (scratched, injured, traded, or not yet called up). The power-play view does the same with 5-on-4 units (top five by 5-on-4 time = first unit, next five = second; 30+ seconds needed). Rows are sorted by usual slot, forwards first. Names link to the player's page.

What this can and can't see
  • Slots come from ice time, not the coach's posted line chart. Penalties, injuries during a game and blowouts can shuffle one night, so read the pattern over several games, not a single square.
  • Three forwards with nearly the same ice time can land on different "lines" by a few seconds, which is why middle-six forwards often flicker between 2nd and 3rd.

Is each player used well?

Regulars with games in at least 25% of the team's games. Used as is his average slot with this team; Last 10 (or 20) is the same over the team's last 10 games, with an arrow when it differs by three-quarters of a slot or more. The recent column describes his role only: it doesn't feed his value or any fit rating, because 10 games is too few. The fit columns (5v5 fit, overall fit, vs. role, the even-strength flag, power-play and penalty-kill use) are the same as the team deployment card on the skater page and are computed from three seasons, most recent counting most. The "usage flags" tile on the card counts how many regulars are used about right, could do more, or are used above their overall value.

What this can and can't see
  • It compares usage with results; it isn't a verdict on the coach. Injuries, matchups, linemates and development plans all shape who plays where.
  • A player traded mid-season shows his games and slots with this team only, but his fit columns use his whole season.
  • When a team changed coaches, the season's usage is both coaches together.

Who carries the team

Every player who played for the team that season, placed by age (as of February 1) and wins above replacement from the value model. Dot size is games with this team. A player traded mid-season counts only his share of games here (wins × games with this team ÷ his games that season), and every team is split the same way for the ranks. The tiles show the team's total wins and rank, how much of its positive wins come from players 25 and under, the top three players' share, and the average age weighted by games.

Pay vs. value: type any player's cap hit to compare it with his value for a full season at his rate (the same figure as his player page). Cap hits typed here stay on this page only and clear on refresh. They don't read or change the ones saved on player pages or typed in the trade machine.

What this can and can't see
  • The site doesn't know contracts, so the roster's real payroll isn't shown; only cap hits you type are compared.
  • The model prices every win the same, so stars often look underpaid next to the market (see the value model limits).
  • A player with few games has a shaky value a season; it's his rate, not a full season's proof.

Goalies · goalies.html

Goalie page basics

Goalies have their own page with the same four parts, because almost nothing a skater is judged on applies to them. Searching a goalie's name on the skater page sends you there, and the other way around. Goalies are only compared with goalies.

Pools: Regulars have at least 25% of the season's games (minimum 5); Starters have started at least 45% of them. Percentiles work exactly as for skaters, flipped where lower is better (GAA, rebounds).

Save % = 1 − goals against ÷ shots on goal Expected save % = 1 − expected goals against ÷ shots on goal Sv% vs expected (pts) = 100 × (expected goals against − goals against) ÷ shots on goal GSAx = expected goals against − goals against GSAx/60 = GSAx ÷ minutes × 60 Danger sv% vs exp. = 100 × (xGA − GA) ÷ shots, within MoneyPuck's low, mid or high danger group Rebounds vs exp. /60 = (rebounds allowed − expected rebounds) ÷ hours (lower is better) Freezes vs exp. /60 = (pucks frozen − expected freezes) ÷ hours

Expected goals against include missed shots, which can't score, so they're compared with shots on goal for the expected save %. Empty-net time doesn't count: the goalie is on the bench.

Limits: MoneyPuck's expected-goals model knows shot location, type, angle, rebounds and rushes, but not screens, deflections in front or passes right before the shot. Goalies behind teams that allow a lot of those are shortchanged a little. And over one season, save % is roughly half skill and half luck, so single-season goalie numbers swing far more than skater numbers.

Goalie composite and stat board

Stopping = avg(GSAx/60, Sv% vs expected, 5v5 GSAx/60) Danger & situations = avg(high-, mid- and low-danger sv% vs expected, PK GSAx/60, rebounds vs expected) Workload = avg(starts, shots faced /60, xGA/60 faced) Composite = weighted average of the three, using the sliders (default 50 / 30 / 20)

Workload is the goalie version of role difficulty: carrying the crease and facing a heavy, dangerous load is harder than a light backup schedule. Presets: Balanced, Pure saves (no workload) and Workhorse.

Stat board: the same color-coded percentile matrix as for skaters, with workload, results, danger, situation, puck-control and record columns. "Most similar" uses the root-mean-square percentile difference across the save and workload columns (not wins or shutouts, which depend on the team).

Head to head: a percentile radar (overall, 5v5, high/mid/low danger, PK, rebound control and workload) and a side-by-side table. Expected save % and xGA/60 describe the shots he faced, so they aren't marked as wins.

Shot environment

Left: xGA/60 faced against GSAx/60, so you can see whether a goalie's numbers came behind a strong or a leaky defense. Right: shots on goal per 60 against expected goals per unblocked shot, which separates volume from danger. Lines mark the medians (and zero GSAx).

Shot maps

Built from the same MoneyPuck shot files as skater maps, but grouped by the goalie who faced each shot: 5 × 5 ft cells across his defensive zone, for all situations, 5v5 and the penalty kill. Where shots came from compares his share of shots from each spot with the league total for all goalies (orange = more often than average; full color at 0.25 percentage points per cell, since a goalie's mix of shots stays close to the league's). Saves vs expected shows expected goals minus goals allowed per shot (blue = better than average from there; full color at ±0.06 per shot). Drawn smoothed by default, the same way as skater maps; Cells shows the raw cells (5+ shots for saves vs expected).

Goalie swap

His rate = (xGA − GA) ÷ xGA this season (goals saved per expected goal) Goals against there = the replaced goalie's xGA × (1 − his rate) Goals saved = replaced goalie's actual goals against − goals against there Wins ≈ goals saved ÷ 6

Using the rate per expected goal (rather than per 60) makes his saves scale with how many chances the new team actually allows. The reverse swap is shown too.

Limits: assumes save talent travels unchanged and the team in front of him plays the same. Goalie systems, rebound handling and puck-playing interact with the defense in ways this doesn't capture.

Workload and fit

Starts and decisions come from the NHL's game-by-game goalie logs; shots, goals and expected goals from MoneyPuck's game-by-game goalie file.

Start share = his starts ÷ his team's games (while he was on each team, if traded) Role 65%+ workhorse starter · 50%+ starter · 40–50% 1A · 28–40% 1B · 12–28% backup · under 12% spot duty Results fit = GSAx/60 blended over 3 seasons (weights 3, 2, 1 × minutes), ranked among this season's regular goalies: top third = starter results · middle = tandem · bottom = backup Flag results level above his role → "Results earn more starts" below → "Starting more than his results support"

Rest: each start is grouped by days since his previous start: back-to-back (started yesterday too), 1, 2 or 3+ days of rest, with save %, expected save % and GSAx/60, next to the league rate for the same rest. Back-to-backs also count the team's second nights and how many he started. Quality starts use the Hockey Prospectus definition in the glossary. Pulled counts starts where another goalie came in and he played under 55 minutes. Home and road starts are split too.

Goalie value and fair contract

Goals above replacement, for each situation (5v5, penalty kill, everything else) = 0.5 × (GSAx − replacement rate × hours in that situation) Replacement rate = 20th percentile GSAx/60 among regular goalies, in that situation Wins = goals ÷ 6 Price of a goalie win = 32 × (cap × 0.12 goalie share − 2 goalies × minimum salary) ÷ all positive goalie wins that season Worth = minimum salary + max(0, his wins this season) × price of a goalie win (an unfinished season is projected at his pace) Worth over last 3 seasons = the same, using his wins a season blended over that season and the two before (weights 3, 2, 1, an unfinished season weighted by how much is played)

Why half credit: GSAx is the goalie's own number, but a season of it is roughly half signal and half noise and team effects the model can't see, so only half is credited. That also keeps goalie wins on a scale comparable with skater wins: in a typical season goalies produce about 13–15% of all positive wins, close to their 12% share of the cap. (Dollar worth barely depends on this weight: doubling everyone's wins would halve the price of a win.)

Why not per 82 games: skater worth uses wins per 82, because skaters play most games. No goalie plays 82, so goalie worth uses the wins he actually produced, and projections use his own workload.

Rate = wins per 82 games, 3-season blend (weights 3, 2, 1 × games) Kept share = shots faced in those seasons ÷ (shots faced + 3,000) Projected = league-average goalie rate + kept share × (his rate − league-average rate) Workload = his games per season, same 3/2/1 blend (up to 65; an unfinished season counts in proportion to how much of it is played) Wins a year = projected rate × workload ÷ 82, then the age factor for each year Age factors ≤24 ×1.08 · 25–27 ×1.03 · 28–31 ×1.00 · 32–33 ×0.95 · 34–35 ×0.90 · 36+ ×0.85

Trusting repeated results: one season as a starter (about 1,600 shots) keeps roughly a third of his edge over an average goalie; three full seasons (about 4,800) keep roughly 60%. So a single hot season doesn't project like an established elite goalie, while three strong seasons in a row still do. A call-up with a few hundred shots projects close to average. The 3,000-shot setting (GOALIE_SHOTS_K) is a judgment call, roughly where save % is as much skill as luck. Skaters keep their own, simpler projection.

Goalies peak later and hold it longer than skaters, so the age curve is shifted. Fair AAV for a term is the average projected worth over those years, as for skaters. The trade machine uses the same projection, in wins per season.

Limits: the noise in goalie seasons means projections are much less certain than for skaters; the pull toward average only partly handles it, and the workload assumes his recent share of starts continues. Contracts aren't known; type a cap hit to compare.

Goalie careers, arcs and comps

League save % (each season) = 1 − goals against ÷ shots, all goalies with shot counts Save % vs league (×1000) = 1000 × (his save % − league save %) GSAA = shots × (1 − league save %) − goals against Era-adjusted GAA = GAA × 3.0 ÷ that season's league GAA

The NHL has recorded shots against since 1955-56, so save % and GSAA start there; earlier seasons have record, shutouts and GAA only. Expected goals (GSAx) start in 2008-09. "Same age" and "same career stage" work as for skaters, with a full season being 20+ games.

Comps: goalies with at least 40 games (or half of his total) by his age, compared on save % vs league (weight 1.2, if both have shot counts for most games), era-adjusted GAA (0.8), games played (0.6) and GSAx/60 (1, if both have 50+ hours of 2008+ data), each standardized by its spread.

Limits: wins, shutouts and GAA depend heavily on the team. League save % rose steadily for decades with equipment and coaching, so save % vs league is the fairest cross-era measure here, but it still doesn't know shot quality before 2008-09.

Sources and updates

  • MoneyPuck.com: skater and goalie season summaries, lines and pairs, game-by-game skater and goalie data, and shot data from 2008-09 on. Free for non-commercial use with credit to MoneyPuck.com.
  • NHL stats API: every skater and goalie season since 1917-18, bios, league scoring, and goalie game logs (starts and decisions).
  • NHL player and roster API: trophies (including the Vezina and Jennings), Hall of Fame, current rosters and draft picks.
  • NHL game-center data: each game's head coaches, for the teams page.
  • NHL EDGE API: skating speed, bursts, distance, shot speed and zone time, 2021-22 on.

A GitHub Actions job rebuilds the data every morning (about 7 a.m. Eastern) after MoneyPuck's overnight update. Past seasons are kept; the current season is refreshed each day. A new season (from September 1) is picked up automatically once MoneyPuck and the NHL publish it.
